The need

Building skills for calmer, healthier communities.

Emotional resilience and conflict skills are the ability to respond to stressful or unexpected situations, and to react appropriately to the problem in front of you. They affect everyone — regardless of age, identity or life experience.

The real-world problem

Conflict is normal. Handling it well is a skill.

Disagreements and hurt feelings are an ordinary part of growing up. They happen in every school and every year level.

The issue isn't that conflict occurs. It's that students are expected to navigate it without ever being taught how.

Without those skills, a student's options narrow fast. They can't name the feeling. They don't notice frustration building. They have no way to say “this upset me” that doesn't sound like an accusation.

So reactions take over — and a two-minute problem becomes something a teacher has to untangle.

These are learnable skills. They can be taught, practised and strengthened — the same way we teach reading, or swimming, or road safety.

The three problems we're solving

  1. Students struggle to work through problems on their own, so disagreements end up needing teacher intervention.
  2. Younger students find it hard to communicate their emotions — they feel something strongly but can't put it into words.
  3. Students react before they think, because regulating a strong emotion in the moment is a skill they haven't been taught.

None of these describe a problem child. They describe a gap in skills — and gaps in skills can be closed.

What schools do now

The current approach is reactive.

Schools already do a lot here, and they do it well. Most rely on some combination of:

  • A trusted adult students can approach for help
  • A reflection space where a student can reset
  • Short learning sessions on regulation strategies
  • A structured conversation once emotions have settled

Every one of these is valuable. But they mostly happen after something has gone wrong — and they depend on an adult being available.

Mind Matters works the other way around: it gives students the strategies before the moment arrives, and lets them practise until the strategies feel usable on their own.

Target audience

Who Mind Matters is for.

Students are who the workshop is for. Schools are who we work with.

Who takes part

Year 3–6 students, roughly ages 8–12.

  • Old enough to reflect on their own reactions
  • Navigating increasingly complex friendships
  • Often never explicitly taught what to do with big feelings

Who books it

Primary schools and K–12 schools with Year 3–6 students.

  • Principals and deputy principals
  • Wellbeing coordinators and student support staff
  • Heads of year and classroom teachers

Where we're starting

In the early stages we're focused on independent and private schools with Year 3–6 students, who are best placed to fund a workshop directly. Longer term, we want to pursue government funding and grants so public schools can run it just as easily — cost shouldn't decide which students get these skills.

This shapes everything: the activities are pitched for upper primary, while the practical detail school staff need — timing, group size, cost, what's included — is set out plainly on For Schools.

Why Year 3–6

Upper primary is a practical window.

Ages 8–12 is the sweet spot: mature enough to understand the topic properly, early enough that what they learn becomes habit rather than correction.

Friendships get more complex

Social groups shift, loyalties change, and disagreements carry more weight than they did in the early years.

Self-reflection is developing

Students at this age can begin to notice their own reactions and consider perspectives other than their own.

Habits are still forming

Early enough that strategies are retained and built on, rather than having to undo a pattern later.

Step one

The need is already there in primary school.

These three Australian findings are about primary-aged students specifically. They're why the workshop targets Years 3–6.

Primary-aged · Australia

56%

of Australian Year 4 students reported experiencing bullying monthly or weekly

Higher than the figure for Year 8 students (43%). Conflict between students doesn't begin in secondary school.

Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, Australia's children (Bullying), reporting TIMSS 2015 data. aihw.gov.au

Primary-aged · Australia

13.6%

of Australian children aged 4–11 experienced a mental disorder in the previous 12 months

Wellbeing needs are present in the primary years — not something that arrives later with adolescence.

Lawrence, D. et al. (2015), The Mental Health of Children and Adolescents, Australian Government Department of Health. health.gov.au

Primary-aged · Australia

46.3%

of 4–11 year olds with a mental disorder had used a health service for it

Compared with 60.7% of 12–17 year olds. The youngest students are the least likely to be connected to support — which is exactly why teaching these skills to a whole class matters.

To be clear: these are not statistics about Year 3–6 students, and we don't present them as such. They describe where the need goes when it isn't met early — and they're the reason we think it's worth starting at eight, not eighteen.

Step three

The good news: these skills respond to teaching.

A landmark meta-analysis of 213 school-based social and emotional learning programs, covering 270,034 students from kindergarten to high school, found participants showed improved social and emotional skills, attitudes, behaviour and academic performance — a gain equivalent to 11 percentile points in achievement.

Durlak, J. A. et al. (2011), Child Development, 82(1), 405–432. View study. This covers all school ages across multiple countries and evaluates structured SEL programs generally — not Mind Matters.

Emotional resilience and conflict skills aren't fixed traits. They can be learned and strengthened — through self-awareness, emotional awareness and regulation. That's what a workshop can realistically start.

What shapes it

Emotional regulation isn't fixed.

A student's ability to manage and respond to an emotional situation shifts depending on what else is going on for them. Known factors include:

  • Sleep — a tired student has far less capacity to self-regulate
  • Stress — pressure at home or school narrows the window
  • Mental health conditions — which can make regulation harder day to day
  • Support — whether there's a trusted adult nearby, and whether the student will use them

Source: Emotion Regulation Clinic, Floreat WA.

This is why we teach strategies rather than rules. A student having a hard week needs something they can actually reach for.

Empathy interviews

What we heard

Before designing anything, we put twelve questions to Year 3–6 teachers about what conflict actually looks like in their classrooms. Their answers shaped the whole workshop.

What the interviews told us

Where conflict comes from

  • Disagreements over games and rules, especially at break times
  • Perceived unfairness — feeling left out, or that a friend is choosing someone else
  • Miscommunication, or comments taken the wrong way
  • Competitiveness around sport and achievement
  • By Year 6, loyalty and shifting friendship groups rather than the original disagreement

How students respond

  • Some shut down or withdraw; others become tearful
  • Some react outwardly — raised voices, blaming, storming off
  • Many can't yet name the feeling, so it surfaces as behaviour instead
  • Students often don't know how to ask for help
  • Small disputes repeat, because the underlying issue isn't understood
1

The gap is staying calm, not knowing what to do. Students can often name the right strategy — accessing it mid-conflict is the hard part.

2

Teaching happens reactively. Most conflict skills are taught after something has already gone wrong, rather than built in advance.

3

Perspective-taking is the hardest skill. Taking responsibility and seeing the other person's side is where students struggle most.

4

Practice beats explanation. Students are far more likely to use a strategy under stress if they've already rehearsed it while calm.

5

Role-play and scenarios work best. Interactive, low-pressure and hands-on activities get better engagement than direct instruction.

6

Year 3 and Year 6 need different things. Younger students need concrete visuals and shorter activities; older students need nuance and ownership.

How this shaped the workshop

Both teachers independently named the same core skills: recognising and naming emotions including the physical signs of escalation, a toolkit of calming strategies students can use alone, “I” statements, active listening and perspective-taking, and a simple repeatable process for working through a disagreement. That list became the workshop's four steps — and the reason regulation comes before resolution.

These are qualitative findings from interviews with Year 3–6 teachers. They reflect the perspectives of a small number of educators, not statistically representative data.

Where it fits

Aligned with what schools are already asked to teach.

These skills aren't an add-on to the Australian curriculum — they're already named in it.

The Personal and Social Capability (Version 9) is built on four elements: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness and social management. Emotional regulation sits under self-management. Empathy sits under social awareness.

The Australian Student Wellbeing Framework, endorsed by education ministers, likewise positions student wellbeing and positive relationships as central to learning, through its five elements of leadership, inclusion, student voice, partnerships and support.

Sources: ACARA — Personal and Social Capability (V9); Australian Government Department of Education — Australian Student Wellbeing Framework.

What Mind Matters is — and isn't

It is: an educational, skill-building workshop that gives students practical tools to recognise, regulate, communicate and resolve.

It isn't: a counselling service, a diagnostic tool, a clinical mental health intervention, or a guarantee that conflict or bullying will stop.

We use research to explain why these skills matter. We don't use it to claim outcomes the workshop can't promise.

The skills don't stop at the school gate.

A student who can notice frustration, take a moment and say what they need has a tool for life — high school, a first job, friendships, their own family one day. Teaching it at ten is early. That's the point.
